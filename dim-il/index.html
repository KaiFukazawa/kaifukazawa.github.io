Density-Informed Margin
Imitation Learning (DIM-IL)

Can a policy learn to stay near expert behavior using demonstrations alone?

DIM-IL augments behavior cloning with a support-aware training signal. It estimates where expert states lie, then discourages actions predicted to move the policy into low-support regions, without requiring online expert corrections.

Kai Fukazawa University of California, Davis

01 / The problem

Small errors can take a policy somewhere its data cannot guide it.

Behavior cloning learns directly from fixed expert demonstrations. That simplicity makes it scalable, but an imperfect action can shift the next state away from the training distribution. Errors can compound as the policy continues from states it has rarely seen.

Interactive imitation learning can collect corrections on those states. For physical robots, however, additional rollouts and expert intervention can be expensive or risky. DIM-IL asks whether offline demonstrations already contain enough information to shape safer behavior.

02 / The method

From imitation to support-aware imitation.

The training objective keeps behavior cloning as its foundation and adds a soft penalty for predicted support-margin violations.

01

Estimate expert-state support

Fit a normalizing flow to the states in expert demonstrations. Its log density provides a signal for whether a state resembles those the expert visited.

02

Predict support drift

Learn a one-step model that estimates how a candidate action changes the support score. This converts state density into an action-level signal.

03

Shape the policy

Train the policy with behavior cloning plus a soft margin penalty. Actions predicted to leave expert-supported states receive a larger penalty.

The margin is soft: the goal is to prefer support-preserving actions, not to impose a hard constraint that every predicted step must satisfy.

04 / Next steps

Beyond a single manipulation task.

The planned evaluation extends to harder, longer-horizon manipulation, including ALOHA simulation and hardware tasks with image observations and bimanual control.

Current results are a starting point for testing whether the same support-aware signal remains useful as observations and actions become more complex.
